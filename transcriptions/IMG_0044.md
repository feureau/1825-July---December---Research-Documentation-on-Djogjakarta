## Key Events & Entities

### Key Events
- **Nahuys' Rebuttal Regarding Royal Guardianship:** Nahuys points out that if Diponegoro had truly defied Sultan Hamengkubuwana IV and refused palace summonses, the Sultan would have reported this insolence immediately. Furthermore, upon HB IV's death in 1822, his mother (Ratu Ibu) and relatives explicitly requested that Diponegoro be appointed co-guardian (*mede-voogd*) of the young child-Sultan Hamengkubuwana V—a step impossible had there been open enmity.
- **Material Interest as Driver of Javanese Politics:** Nahuys introduces a general historical maxim: among the Javanese, love or hatred toward Europeans is primarily the product of their self-interest (*eigenbelang*).
- **Historical Precedents of Resistance and Settlement:** Nahuys draws a direct parallel between Diponegoro's rebellion and earlier 18th-century royal rebels:
  1. *Pangeran Mangkubumi* (later Sultan Hamengkubuwana I, founder of Yogyakarta).
  2. *Raden Mas Said* (later Pangeran Adipati Mangkunagara I).

## Document Summary

In this continuation of his critique (p. 15), Nahuys van Burgst dismantles the colonial thesis of Diponegoro's long-standing, fanatical enmity toward Europeans. Nahuys notes that when Sultan Hamengkubuwana IV died unexpectedly in December 1822, the Sultan's mother and close family personally asked the colonial government to name Diponegoro co-guardian of the infant Hamengkubuwana V. This family consensus decisively refutes Raden Mas Alip's claim that Diponegoro was an outcast or estranged rival at court.

Nahuys then articulates a realistic political philosophy regarding Javanese aristocratic behavior: alignment with or opposition to European authority is driven by concrete material interest (*eigenbelang*) rather than abstract hatred. To demonstrate this, Nahuys invokes the celebrated historical precedents of Prince Mangkubumi (Hamengkubuwana I) and Raden Mas Said (Mangkunagara I), who waged ferocious wars against the Dutch East India Company (VOC) when their rights were trampled, but became staunch and faithful rulers once their positions, dignity, and domains were guaranteed.

## Contextual & Historiographical Notes

1. **Dynastic Nuances and Terminology:**
   Nahuys refers to Diponegoro as the "onechten broeder" (illegitimate brother) of Hamengkubuwana IV. Under Javanese royal custom, Diponegoro was the firstborn son of Hamengkubuwana III by an official concubine (*garwa ampeyan*, Raden Ayu Mangkarawati), whereas Hamengkubuwana IV was born of the queen consort (*garwa padmi*, Ratu Ibu / Ratu Kencono). While Dutch legalism often translated *selir/ampeyan* as "illegitimate," Javanese custom conferred full princely dignity (*Pangeran*), and Diponegoro's moral stature at court was exceptionally high.
2. **Comparison with Hamengkubuwana I and Mangkunagara I:**
   Nahuys' contextualization of Diponegoro within the tradition of Mangkubumi and Raden Mas Said is insightful. Both heroes of the Third Javanese War of Succession (1746–1757) fought tenaciously against VOC encroachment, establishing dynasties that later co-existed with European authority. Nahuys implies that Diponegoro was driven into rebellion by administrative blundering, broken treaties, and economic dispossession rather than an irrational, ancestral fanaticism.
