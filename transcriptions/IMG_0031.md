## Contextual Notes & Analysis
- **The Salt Monopoly & Toll Extortion (7 Jan 1825):** Salt was a vital everyday necessity and a strictly controlled state monopoly. Under the British administration (1811–1816), salt flowed freely from Gresik into the interior. Following the 1816 Dutch restoration, the proliferation of private Chinese toll-gates (*bandar*) multiplied transit duties, driving up salt prices to catastrophic levels in the Vorstenlanden. Poor peasants traveled as far as the government depot in Salatiga to purchase salt, while riverborne transport along the Bengawan Solo was stifled by fiscal barriers.
- **Rural Indebtedness & Royal Retainers (24 Jan 1825):** The debt dispute involving Radèn Ngabèhi Měntadiwiria, a court official supervising the royal transport coolies (*gladhag*) in Surakarta, illustrates how court mantris borrowed cash from Chinese financiers (Tan Tian Hok) to meet royal tax demands on villages such as Kalibawang (in Kulon Progo). Měntadiwiria's inability to service a 660 Spanish dollar debt highlights systemic financial insolvency among Javanese administrative elites.
- **Pass System & Ethnic Segregation (28 Jan 1825):** The colonial decree of 20 May 1823 imposed rigid travel pass requirements (*passenstelsel*) on ethnic Chinese entering the Principalities. While Chinese merchants petitioned Governor-General Van der Capellen for relief, Resident MacGillavry observed that as long as the colonial state leased lucrative tollgates to Chinese tax farmers, suppressing unlicensed Chinese travel and trade was impossible.
